# Already Have Statistical Output? Reporting Help or a New Analysis?

You have a spreadsheet, an output file, and a deadline. What you do not have is a finished results section. Before buying another tool or asking someone to repeat the analysis, identify what is preventing you from finishing. You may need help presenting an existing result, help locating information in the original output, or help deciding whether the analysis addresses your research question.

Those are different tasks. A useful purchase should make clear which task it covers and what you will receive.

## When the immediate task is reporting

Reporting help is the relevant starting point when your question is about communicating an analysis you have already chosen and completed. You might be trying to organize a results paragraph, understand the labels in an output file, or find the reporting guidance for your method.

Write down the specific obstacle before choosing a service: “I have the output, but I do not know which part belongs in my results section.” That is more actionable than “I need help with statistics.” Keep the original output available so that any proposed wording can be checked against it.

A reporting example is a guide to presentation. It does not, by itself, establish that your method was suitable or that the underlying analysis was correct. If either point is unresolved, include it explicitly in your request for help.

## When rewriting the paragraph is not enough

If you cannot explain what question the analysis was intended to answer, formatting is not the first decision to make. The same applies when you are unsure which variables or observations were included, or whether the output belongs to the final version of your data.

Describe the uncertainty directly. For example: “I have an analysis file, but I need someone to review whether it addresses this research question.” That request has a different scope from polishing a paragraph. A provider should be able to tell you whether that review is included before you buy.

Do not treat more polished wording as evidence that an unresolved methodological question has been settled.

## When information appears to be missing

Return to the original output before assuming that you must repeat the analysis. Identify what your intended report requires and what you can actually locate. Preserve the labels and context around any values you copy; an isolated number may not explain which result it represents.

If you still cannot find the needed information, ask a precise question: “Where is this information in my output, or does obtaining it require additional analysis?” Whether additional analysis is needed depends on the method and the available material. A generic reporting example cannot answer that question for every project.

Never fill an empty space with a value taken from an example. Keep an illustrative example visibly separate from your own findings.

## Prepare a brief that makes the purchase useful

State your research question in ordinary language. Name the analysis you ran, if you know it, and describe the material you already have. Then specify the deliverable: an explanation of the output, help preparing a results paragraph, or a review of the analysis itself.

Include the reporting requirements you have been given by your department, supervisor, or publication. Ask the provider to distinguish work included in the purchase from decisions that still need your input. You should be able to understand the boundary without guessing from a broad promise of “statistical help.”

If you need to share research material, first check what you are permitted to disclose and what the provider actually needs. Do not assume that buying reporting help automatically requires uploading the complete dataset.

## Check the offer against the task

For an immediate reporting task, inspect the current price, the payment type, and the promised output together. If the purchase uses credits, find out what the intended operation consumes and whether repeating or revising it requires another purchase. If the offer is a subscription, consider whether you have a recurring need.

Look for an example that shows the kind of deliverable provided. Distinguish an explanation, a formatted result, and a review of the analysis: similar wording on a sales page can conceal different scopes. Ask for clarification when the offer does not make that boundary clear.

Avoid choosing solely because a tool lists many methods. The relevant question is whether it can finish the task you currently have, using the material you currently possess.

## Make the next step specific

If the analysis is settled and the obstacle is presentation, seek reporting help for that method. If the analysis itself is uncertain, request methodological review. If information is missing, locate it in the original material or establish whether additional work is required.

The decision is about the unresolved task, not simply whether you already own statistical software. A clear task description gives you a practical way to compare offers and check whether a purchase delivered what you needed.
